\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

% Motivation: generators of valid inputs are essential but hard to construct.
Constructing efficient generators for inputs satisfying a given predicate is
often difficult in property-based testing (PBT)~\cite{claessen00quickcheck}.
Many properties apply only to inputs that satisfy a precondition.
When valid inputs are rare, filtering random inputs leaves few useful test
cases~\cite{lampropoulos18generators}.
Generating only valid inputs avoids filtering but requires satisfying all
predicate constraints by construction.
In interviews, experienced users named writing such generators a main source
of complexity in PBT~\cite{goldstein24practice}.

% Existing techniques: relation-based derivation, its extensions, and deductive synthesis.
Existing techniques automate generator construction.
For example, QuickChick, a PBT tool for Rocq~\cite{paraskevopoulou15foundational},
derives generators from inductive relations~\cite{lampropoulos18generators}.
Paraskevopoulou et al.~\cite{paraskevopoulou22computing} extend this approach
with a common framework for deriving generators and supporting checkers.
Within QuickChick, Mittelman et al.~\cite{mittelman26testing} automate the
choice of generation modes and premise order.
Prinz and Lampropoulos~\cite{prinz23merging} provide relation merging:
combining multiple relations into one equivalent to their conjunction so that
constraints can be handled together before generator derivation.
For Lean, Goldstein et al.~\cite{goldstein26palamedes} introduce Palamedes,
which uses deductive synthesis rules to construct generators from predicates
expressed as functions.

% Example: existing automation remains sensitive to how constraints are expressed.
However, these techniques can fail or yield inefficient generators for some predicates.
Consider \texttt{Goal} in Figure~\ref{fig:intro-reformulation}, which requires
a natural number to satisfy both \texttt{F} (multiples of two) and
\texttt{G} (multiples of three).
The derivation of Paraskevopoulou et al.\ processes premises in sequence.
For \texttt{Goal}, it draws \texttt{x} satisfying \texttt{F x},
then discards it if \texttt{G x} does not hold.
Mittelman et al.'s approach still generates each variable from at most one
precondition and checks other constraints afterward.
Reversing the premises draws \texttt{x} satisfying
\texttt{G x} and checks \texttt{F x}.
We compared QuickChick generators derived from these two fixed premise orders.
In 500,000 calls per generator (five seeds, size 30),
the original and reversed orders returned no value in 64.5\% and
48.6\% of calls, respectively.
Relation merging can also leave constraints separate when their recursive
structures do not align.
Merging \texttt{F} and \texttt{G} yields \texttt{Merged}; its generator's nonzero
branch draws \texttt{x} satisfying \texttt{G x} and checks \texttt{F (S x)}.
Failed checks fall back to zero: this generator always returned a value in the
same experiment, but 75.8\% of its outputs were zero.
Palamedes can fail to synthesize a generator when its rules cannot handle a
recursive predicate's form or a required predefined generator is missing.
With its default deductive rules, it produced no generator for a Lean version
of \texttt{Goal}.

% Opportunity: prior agent work motivates exploring how agents can assist generator construction.
Agents offer a new way to approach generator construction for predicates that
existing techniques struggle to handle.
Constructing generators for such predicates often requires reasoning beyond
the syntactic form of their constraints.
For example, Goldstein et al.\ rewrite a sortedness predicate using a different
recursion scheme to make their synthesis rules applicable~\cite{goldstein26palamedes}.
Prior work uses agents to identify properties and write generators from code
and documentation~\cite{maaz25agentic}, and to construct proofs in interactive
theorem provers~\cite{thakur24copra}.
These results suggest that agents may also perform such reasoning.
In the \texttt{Goal} example, the key observation is that the values satisfying both
\texttt{F} and \texttt{G} are exactly the multiples of six.
An agent could use this observation to define an equivalent predicate,
\texttt{Agentic}, with a base case for zero and a recursive case that adds six.
From \texttt{Agentic}, we derived a QuickChick generator that needs no further
constraint checks.
In the same experiment, it returned a valid input on every call, and only
3.2\% of outputs were zero.

% Agent limitations: reliability and scalability constrain generator construction.
However, agentic generator construction faces two challenges:
\emph{reliability} and \emph{scalability}.
Without verification, an agent-constructed generator may be unsound or
incomplete.
An unsound generator produces inputs that violate the predicate, causing false
alarms.
An incomplete generator never produces some valid inputs, possibly missing bugs.
In agentic PBT, where the agent writes both properties and generators, only
56\% of sampled bug reports were valid~\cite{maaz25agentic}.
As predicates grow more complex, construction may require many attempts or fail
within the available budget.
Nondeterministic agent runs make success and the required number of attempts
hard to predict.

% ITP proofs ensure reliability, and tools prove their generators, but the whole task cannot be left to the agent.
Proofs of soundness and completeness in an interactive theorem prover can
address the reliability challenge.
The derivation tools of Lampropoulos et al.~\cite{lampropoulos18generators}
and Goldstein et al.~\cite{goldstein26palamedes} already provide such proofs
automatically for the generators they derive.
An agent could likewise prove its generators sound and complete, but constructing
these proofs may pose an additional challenge.

% Proposal: verified agentic predicate reformulation; the agent proves only equivalence, and QuickChick derives the generator and its proofs.
We therefore propose \emph{verified agentic predicate reformulation} to make
efficient generator construction reliable without assigning the entire task to the agent.
The approach aims to make existing derivation techniques effective on a broader
range of predicates.
The agent reformulates the predicate into a form that admits efficient
generation, while proving predicate equivalence at each step.
QuickChick then derives a generator from the final form
and proves it sound and complete for that
form~\cite{lampropoulos18generators,paraskevopoulou22computing}.
The equivalence proofs extend this guarantee to the original predicate.

% Evaluation: experiments on a SpecTec-based toolchain.
We evaluate the approach through experiments with a SpecTec-based toolchain
for testing language implementations.
SpecTec~\cite{youn24spectec} is a domain-specific language for specifying
programming languages.
Given a SpecTec specification and a property, the toolchain constructs a verified
generator for the precondition and uses it to test external
implementations against the property.

% Central question: intentionally left blank.
% Research questions: intentionally left blank.

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We propose verified agentic predicate reformulation for efficient
    generator construction. The method uses equivalence proofs to extend
    QuickChick's soundness and completeness guarantees to the original predicate.
  \item We instantiate the method in a SpecTec-based toolchain that builds
    verified generators for user-supplied properties' preconditions to test
    external language implementations.
\end{itemize}
