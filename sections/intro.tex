\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

% Motivation.
Efficiently generating diverse inputs that satisfy a given predicate is a
central challenge in property-based testing.

% Existing automation reduces the manual effort of constructing generators.
To reduce the manual effort of constructing such generators, prior works
automate their derivation from formal
predicates~\cite{lampropoulos18generators,mittelman26testing,goldstein26palamedes}.

% Suitable approaches depend on the characteristics of the predicate.
However, each approach is well suited to predicates with particular
characteristics.
Paraskevopoulou et al.~\cite{paraskevopoulou22computing} derive generators by
processing the premises of inductive relations in sequence, but generation can
be inefficient when dependent constraints occur in an unfavorable order.
Prinz and Lampropoulos~\cite{prinz23merging} merge relations to guide generation
with multiple constraints, but merging offers limited benefit when the relations
do not share recursive structure and may require reformulation to expose that
structure.
Mittelman et al.~\cite{mittelman26testing} automatically select generation modes
and premise order.
We note a potential limitation of this approach: its scheduling procedure
generates each variable from at most one precondition, so other constraints on
that variable may still need to be checked after generation.
Goldstein et al.~\cite{goldstein26palamedes} synthesize generators using predefined deductive
rules and transformations of recursive predicates, but synthesis can fail when
the automation cannot convert a predicate into a supported recursive form or
lacks a required predefined generator.
As these limitations show, no single approach is efficient for every predicate.
Constructing an efficient generator therefore requires selecting a suitable approach
for each predicate.
This selection is not obvious because the ideal conditions each approach enjoys,
such as how dependent constraints should be ordered or whether relations share
recursive structure, are often not apparent from the syntax of the predicate.

% Proposed response: agents can adapt their approach to construct efficient generators.
Agents open a new way to construct generators because they can reason about the
semantics of a predicate, not only its syntax.
This lets them apply a suitable strategy for generating inputs that satisfy the
predicate and even find an easier problem to solve, such as a logically equivalent
predicate that admits a more efficient generator.
Prior work has explored using agents for property-based testing of real-world
software~\cite{maaz25agentic}.

% Agent limitations: reliability (illustrated by agentic PBT) and scalability.
However, agentic generator construction faces two challenges:
\emph{reliability} and \emph{scalability}.
Without verification, an agent-constructed generator may be unsound or
incomplete.
An unsound generator produces inputs that violate the predicate, causing false
alarms.
An incomplete generator never produces some valid inputs, possibly missing bugs.
Unverified agent output undermines reliability in practice: in agentic PBT,
where the agent writes both properties and generators, only 56\% of sampled bug
reports were valid~\cite{maaz25agentic}.
As specifications and properties grow more complex, finding efficient generation
strategies and constructing their correctness proofs become more demanding.
An agent may therefore require many attempts or fail within the available budget.
Moreover, because agent runs are nondeterministic, how many attempts
construction needs and whether it succeeds are hard to predict.

% ITP proofs can ensure reliability but worsen the scalability challenge.
Of these two challenges, reliability can be addressed by formalizing generators
in an interactive theorem prover and proving them correct.
\emph{Generating Good Generators} and Palamedes already prove the soundness and
completeness of the generators they derive
automatically~\cite{lampropoulos18generators,goldstein26palamedes}.
Likewise, attaching such proofs to agent-constructed generators would guarantee
their correctness.
However, the agent must then construct proofs in addition to generators, which
makes the scalability challenge even harder.

% Proposal: the approach and scaling methods, evaluated with a SpecTec-based toolchain.
This paper proposes an agentic approach to constructing efficient generators and
their correctness proofs.
It also introduces methods for scaling this approach to increasingly complex
specifications and properties.
To evaluate both, we run experiments with a toolchain that we build on top of
SpecTec~\cite{youn24spectec}, a domain-specific language for specifying
programming languages.
In this toolchain, the user provides a language specification in SpecTec and a
property over it, and an agent constructs a generator and its correctness
proofs.
The toolchain then uses the generator to test external implementations of the
language against the property.
